How the model works

Built up one piece at a time; the thrust equation lands in step 5

Numbers quoted in the text below use the default inputs (350 m tether, 25°, L/D 3.5, 1 MN cap). The worked example in step 9 uses whatever the sliders are set to.

The page answers one question: for a kite of area \(A\), how much along-track pull \(T\) does the MT-50 get at a given point and month, and what is that worth over a year? Each step adds one piece of physics. The model is the circle-centre version of Traut et al. (2014), which treats the kite as massless and flying fast loops across the wind.

1Wind at kite height

ERA5 reports wind 10 m above the sea. The kite flies at the end of an \(L = 350\) m tether at elevation \(\varepsilon = 25^\circ\), so it sits at

\[ h = L\sin\varepsilon = 350\,\sin 25^\circ \approx 148\ \text{m}. \]

Wind over open water strengthens with height. A power-law profile is the standard engineering fit:

\[ V(h) = V_{10}\left(\frac{h}{10\ \text{m}}\right)^{\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = 0.11 \text{ offshore}. \]

Here \((148/10)^{0.11} \approx 1.345\). The kite sees about 35% more wind than the spreadsheet shows, and because force scales with speed squared, that is roughly 80% more force.

2Apparent wind on a moving ship

Treat wind as a velocity vector: the direction the air moves toward. The ship moves at \(V_s = 14.5\) kn \(= 7.46\) m/s on heading \(\psi\). Relative to the ship, the air moves at

\[ \vec V_a = \vec V_w(h) - \vec V_s, \qquad V_{a,E} = u - V_s\sin\psi, \quad V_{a,N} = v - V_s\cos\psi, \]

where \(u, v\) are the east and north components of the true wind at kite height. Call \(\delta\) the angle between the direction the apparent wind blows toward and the ship's heading: \(\delta = 0\) is a dead tailwind, \(90^\circ\) a beam wind, \(180^\circ\) a head wind. In a dead tailwind the ship runs away from its own wind, \(V_a = V_w - V_s\), so a 15 kn tailwind at kite height leaves the kite only 0.5 kn. A beam wind keeps all of \(V_w\) and adds the ship's own headwind on top.

3Where the kite sits

The kite flies downwind of the ship in the apparent wind. In plan view its bearing is swung from the apparent downwind direction toward the heading by an azimuth angle \(\varphi\). With axes \(x\) = apparent downwind, \(y\) = crosswind, \(z\) = up, the unit vector along the tether is

\[ \hat r = (\cos\varepsilon\cos\varphi,\ \cos\varepsilon\sin\varphi,\ \sin\varepsilon). \]

The apparent wind is horizontal along \(x\), so the part of it that points along the tether is

\[ V_r = \vec V_a\cdot\hat r = V_a\cos\varepsilon\cos\varphi. \]

Step 4 shows why \(V_r\) is the only part of the wind that loads the tether.

4Why a crosswind kite pulls so hard

The kite flies fast loops across the wind with the tether length held fixed. Following Loyd (1980), treat it as massless and quasi-steady. With a fixed tether there is no radial motion, so the radial part of the air velocity at the kite stays \(V_r\). The kite's own crosswind speed \(v_k\) adds a component perpendicular to the tether, so the airspeed over the canopy is

\[ V_k^2 = V_r^2 + v_k^2. \]

A massless kite carries no net force except the tether, so the aerodynamic resultant (lift \(L\) perpendicular to \(V_k\), drag \(D\) along it) must point straight along the tether. In the figure, the angle between \(V_k\) and the tether equals the angle between \(D\) and the resultant, which gives

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{v_k}{V_r} = \frac{L}{D} = \frac{C_L}{C_D}. \]

The kite accelerates until its crosswind speed is \(L/D\) times the radial wind. Substituting:

\[ V_k^2 = V_r^2\left(1 + \frac{C_L^2}{C_D^2}\right). \]

The tether force equals the aerodynamic resultant:

\[ F = \tfrac12\rho A\sqrt{C_L^2 + C_D^2}\;V_k^2 = \underbrace{\tfrac12\rho A\sqrt{C_L^2+C_D^2}\left(1+\frac{C_L^2}{C_D^2}\right)}_{K}\;V_r^2 . \]

With Traut's kite (\(C_L = 1.0\), \(C_D = 0.286\), so \(L/D = 3.5\)) and \(\rho = 1.2\) kg/m³, the bracket \(1 + 3.5^2 = 13.2\). A kite flying loops pulls about 13 times harder than the same canopy parked in the same wind. \(K = 8.25\ \text{N/(m/s)}^2\) per m² of canopy, or 4,127 for a 500 m² kite.

5From tether force to thrust

The tether pulls the bow along \(\hat r\). Its horizontal part is \(F\cos\varepsilon\), and that makes angle \(\delta - \varphi\) with the heading (plan view above). Using \(V_r\) from step 3:

\[ T = F\cos\varepsilon\cos(\delta-\varphi) = K\,V_a^2\cos^3\varepsilon\,\cos^2\varphi\,\cos(\delta-\varphi). \]

The azimuth is a trade. At \(\varphi = 0\) the kite sits dead downwind and pulls hardest, but at a beam reach all of that pull is sideways. At \(\varphi = \delta\) the kite sits dead ahead and every newton points along track, but the radial wind shrinks. The model uses the halfway rule \(\varphi = \delta/2\), as Traut does, which gives the one-line result

\[ \boxed{\,T = K\,V_a^2\,\cos^3\varepsilon\,\cos^3\!\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\,} \]

Setting \(\partial T/\partial\varphi = 0\) gives the exact optimum, \(\tan(\delta - \varphi) = 2\tan\varphi\). It is about 9% better at a beam reach, more with the wind further forward, and identical in a tailwind. Set AZIMUTH_RULE: "optimal" in the code to use it. The sideways part of the pull, \(F\cos\varepsilon\sin(\delta-\varphi)\), is what the hull and rudder have to resist.

6When the kite can't fly

  • Wind too far forward. If \(\delta > 135^\circ\) the apparent wind comes from within 45° of the bow, the kite can't hold station downwind of the ship, and \(T = 0\).
  • Too little wind. Below \(V_a = 3\) m/s at the kite it stays packed.
  • Tether limit. \(F\) is capped at 1 MN. The default is to depower (reduce angle of attack and hold \(F\) at the cap). Traut lands the kite instead whenever \(F\) would exceed the cap; switch between the two in the Tether limit box at the top. With landing, a bigger kite can do worse because it hits the cap in exactly the windy hours that pay the most.

7A month of wind isn't one number

For each waypoint and month the sheet gives the scalar mean speed \(S\) (the average of hourly speeds) and the vector mean \((U, V)\). Their ratio, the steadiness, is about 40% on this route: the direction swings a lot within a month. Because \(T\) grows with \(V_a^2\) and switches off in the no-fly sector, plugging one monthly mean into the thrust equation underpredicts badly and misses the hours when the kite does fly.

The model treats each hour's 10 m wind as the monthly mean vector plus a random 2-D Gaussian kick with equal spread \(\sigma\) on both axes:

\[ (u, v) \sim \mathcal N\!\big((U, V),\ \sigma^2 I\big), \]

so the speed follows a Rice distribution. \(\sigma\) is the one value that makes the mean speed match the sheet,

\[ \mathbb E\!\left[\sqrt{u^2 + v^2}\,\right] = S, \]

solved numerically for every waypoint and month. The expected thrust is the average of \(T\) over 512 fixed sample points (a Halton sequence pushed through Box–Muller). Those 512 points are the dots on the dial.

8Along the route, over a year

The route is great-circle legs between the six waypoints, 4,836 nm in total. Between waypoints \(U\), \(V\) and \(\sigma\) are interpolated linearly with distance, and the heading at each point is the great-circle course (westbound adds 180°). The annual mean thrust for each leg averages 120 equally spaced points times 12 months, so every mile counts equally. Then

\[ P = \frac{T\,V_s}{\eta_D}, \qquad \text{saving} = \frac{P}{6{,}970\ \text{kW}}, \]

where 6,970 kW is 85% MCR, the MT-50's power at 14.5 kn. The default \(\eta_D = 1\) is conservative: it ignores the propeller's efficiency, which roughly offsets the drift and rudder drag the model leaves out. Setting ETA_D: 0.7 credits the propeller and raises every saving by about 40%. Fuel saved per year:

\[ m_\text{fuel} = \bar P_\text{rt}\; t_\text{sail}\;\text{SFOC}, \qquad t_\text{sail} = 2\cdot\frac{4836\ \text{nm}}{14.5\ \text{kn}}\cdot 10\ \text{trips} \approx 6{,}670\ \text{h}. \]

9The numbers at your selected point

This follows steps 1–6 for the typical condition at the point and month picked above: the monthly mean speed blowing from the dominant direction. It updates with the sliders.

10What it leaves out

  • Tether weight, sag and air drag. Without them a longer tether only adds altitude and never costs anything, so the tether-length slider overstates the gain from a longer line.
  • Kite mass and gravity, and the 10° flight circle (the model evaluates its centre; Traut's step-by-step version is within about 4%).
  • Gusts and dynamic peak loads. Size the tether from peaks, not these means.
  • Leeway, rudder drag and wave-induced ship motion.
  • Real hour-to-hour wind. The Gaussian scatter is an assumption, and it is the biggest uncertainty here: V1's hour-by-hour simulation came in lower than this. Rerun with hourly ERA5 at 100 m before putting numbers in the report.
  • Launch and recovery time, port time and speed changes (the ship always makes 14.5 kn).

11Changing everything else

Every fixed value sits in one CONFIG block at the top of the script; edit it in the HTML file and reload. The values marked slider are only start values. The SLIDERS block right below CONFIG sets each slider's range and whether it starts locked.
